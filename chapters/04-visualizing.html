Chapter 4 · Visualization

Visualizing Latent Spaces

A 64-number code can't be drawn. So we squash it into 2 or 3 dimensions with PCA, t-SNE or UMAP. Each method squashes differently, and each one can fool you. Let's learn what they keep, what they throw away, and how to read them honestly.

Reading time ~15 min

By the end of this chapter you will know

  • the difference between a model trained with 2 latent numbers and a 64-number model projected to 2-D;
  • how PCA, t-SNE and UMAP work, and what explained variance, perplexity, n_neighbors and min_dist do;
  • which visual patterns you can trust (neighbourhoods) and which you can't (cluster sizes and gaps in t-SNE/UMAP).

1.The most important distinction

A 64-dimensional autoencoder does not become a 2-dimensional autoencoder just because we draw it in 2-D.

When we run PCA on 64-number codes, the model has already learned its 64-number representation; we are only taking a picture of it from some angle. A model trained with a 2-number bottleneck is a different thing: it had to throw away everything that didn't fit into 2 numbers. Here, the lesson is simple. Whenever you see a 2-D latent plot, ask: is this the latent space, or a projection of it? In the first case distances are real. In the second case, what you see depends on the method you used.

2.PCA: a straight shadow

Principal Component Analysis finds the directions along which the codes vary the most, and projects onto them. The first principal component (PC1) is the single direction with the largest variance, PC2 the largest remaining one at a right angle to PC1, and so on.

$$ \Sigma = \frac{1}{N}\sum_n (z_n-\bar z)(z_n-\bar z)^\top = V\Lambda V^\top, \qquad y_n = V_{[:,1:k]}^\top (z_n - \bar z) $$
eigenvectors of the covariance matrix are the directions; eigenvalues λj say how much variance each direction carries

Because PCA is linear, distances in the PCA plot are an honest (shrunk) version of real distances. The price is that curved structure gets flattened. To know how much we lose, we look at the explained variance ratio \(\lambda_j / \sum_i \lambda_i\):

3.t-SNE: keep the neighbours together

t-SNE (t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding) doesn't try to keep distances at all. It only tries to keep who is next to whom. In high dimensions it turns distances into "neighbour probabilities" with a Gaussian; in 2-D it does the same with a heavy-tailed Student-t distribution, and moves the points until the two sets of probabilities match:

$$ p_{j|i} \propto \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\lVert z_i-z_j\rVert^2}{2\sigma_i^2}\Big), \qquad q_{ij} \propto \big(1+\lVert y_i-y_j\rVert^2\big)^{-1}, \qquad \min_y \; \mathrm{KL}(P\,\Vert\,Q) $$
σi is chosen per point so that each point has roughly "perplexity" effective neighbours

The heavy tail of the t-distribution pushes moderately distant points far apart in 2-D, which is why t-SNE plots look so clustered. The main knob is perplexity, roughly "how many neighbours each point should care about". Same 64-D codes, three perplexities:

4.UMAP: neighbours on a manifold

UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) starts from the same idea, keep the neighbours, but builds it on the assumption that the data lie on a manifold. It connects each point to its n_neighbors nearest neighbours to build a weighted graph, then finds a 2-D layout with a similar graph. Two knobs matter:

  • n_neighbors: how local the picture is. Small values zoom in on fine structure; large values preserve more global layout.
  • min_dist: how tightly points may pack in 2-D. Small values make tight clumps; large values spread everything out evenly.

And a practical habit: use the same samples and the same colours when you compare projections or models, exactly as in Figures 4.1–4.4. Otherwise you are comparing samples, not methods.

Implementation

from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.manifold import TSNE
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
import umap

z = latents                                          # (N, 64) codes from the encoder (VAE: posterior means)
pca2 = PCA(n_components=2).fit(z - z.mean(0))
print(pca2.explained_variance_ratio_)                 # how much of the picture we keep

x = PCA(n_components=50).fit_transform(StandardScaler().fit_transform(z))   # standard speed-up
tsne2 = TSNE(n_components=2, perplexity=30, init="pca", random_state=42).fit_transform(x)
umap2 = umap.UMAP(n_neighbors=15, min_dist=0.1, random_state=42).fit_transform(x)
Limitations. All projections here use 1,200–4,000 test points, a fixed seed and standardised codes. t-SNE and UMAP results change with all three. For VAEs I plot the posterior means μ, not sampled codes.

Key takeaways

  • A projected 64-D space is a picture of a model, not a 2-D model. Compare both to see the difference.
  • PCA is linear and honest about distances; check the explained variance to know how much it hides.
  • t-SNE and UMAP preserve neighbourhoods. Gaps and cluster sizes are not evidence of anything.
  • Always report perplexity or n_neighbors/min_dist, and compare methods on the same samples.

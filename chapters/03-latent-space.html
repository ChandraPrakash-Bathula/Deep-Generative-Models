Chapter 3 · Latent space

Understanding the Latent Space

The latent code is where all the action is. In this chapter we ask what a single latent number means, what changes when we give the model 2, 8, 16, 64 or 128 of them, and we walk around a real 2-number latent space with the mouse.

Reading time ~14 min

By the end of this chapter you will know

  • what one latent dimension represents, using real models with only 2 dimensions;
  • what each latent size buys you, from extreme compression (2) to high capacity (128);
  • why the numbers inside z are usually not human-interpretable, even when the model is good.

1.An image becomes a point

When the encoder turns a slice into z = (z₁, z₂, …, z_d), we can think of z as the coordinates of a point in a d-dimensional space. Every test slice becomes one point, and the space of all points is the latent space.

slice → encoder → z ∈ ℝd => one point in a d-dimensional space

With d = 64 we can't draw that space. But with d = 2 we can, and that's why I trained real models with only 2 latent numbers. They are the best way to build intuition, so let's start there.

2.What does a single dimension represent?

Here is a simple experiment. Take the 2-number autoencoder, start from an average code, and change only one number at a time, from −2 to +2 standard deviations. Then decode every step.

So the answer to "what does a dimension represent?" is: whatever helps reconstruction the most. With very few dimensions the model is forced to spend them on the biggest sources of variation in the data. For brain slices that is mostly where in the head the slice was cut, together with the size and brightness that go with it. And notice that the model spread that one factor over both axes instead of giving each axis one clean meaning. The next box explains why that's normal.

Then why are latent numbers "not interpretable"?Because the decoder doesn't care which axis is which. If you rotate the whole latent space and rotate the decoder's first layer the opposite way, the outputs are identical. So the loss has no reason to line "height" up with z₁ exactly; usually each axis is a mix of factors. With 2 numbers we got lucky that the mix is readable. With 64 numbers, a single z₃₇ almost never means one clean thing. (Getting clean, one-factor-per-axis codes is a research field of its own, called disentanglement.)

Three things are worth noticing here:

  1. Similar slices sit together. The colours form a smooth band from purple (low slices) to yellow (high slices). The model organised its latent space by anatomy without ever being told about it.
  2. The decoder draws something everywhere, even where there are no dots. In the empty corners it has to guess, and those images are less realistic. Remember this, it is the key to chapter 6.
  3. The shape of the cloud depends on the model. Switch to "dots only" and compare AE with VAE. The AE spreads its codes widely, in long arms with empty space between them (one number reaches +27). VAE β=0.1 roughly halves that spread but is still stretched. VAE β=1 packs them into a compact cloud centred on 0 with a spread of about 1, which is exactly the shape of the prior N(0, 1). Same data, same decoder, different rules.

Why is everything still blurry at 128 numbers?Because of the loss. Mean squared error punishes the average pixel mistake. When the model isn't sure where a fold is, the safest guess is the average of every place it could be, and the average of many sharp images is a blurry one. More latent numbers reduce the uncertainty, so the blur shrinks, but a pixel-wise loss will always prefer a smooth guess to a sharp wrong one.

So how many numbers should you use?

It depends on what you want. For a picture of "how the dataset is organised", 2–3 numbers are perfect, because you can see the space. For good reconstructions, more is better, with diminishing returns: on brains, going from 64 to 128 numbers only bought 0.5 dB. And for a VAE with a large β, extra numbers are simply ignored. In chapter 5 we'll see that faces keep benefiting from more numbers for much longer than brains do.

Limitations. The traversal and plane views only exist for 2-D models; for bigger codes we need projections (next chapter), which bring their own distortions. PSNR on brain slices includes the black background, which inflates every number; the "brain-only PSNR" in Figure 3.3 fixes that.

Key takeaways

  • Each image becomes a point z in ℝd; similar images land close together.
  • With few dimensions, the model spends them on the biggest factors of variation (slice height, head size).
  • Individual latent axes are usually mixtures of factors: the loss doesn't care about rotations of the space.
  • More latent numbers = better reconstructions, with diminishing returns; a pixel loss always leaves some blur.

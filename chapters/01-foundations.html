Chapter 1 · Foundations

Understanding Autoencoders

Before any equation, let's understand the game an autoencoder plays: squeeze an image into a handful of numbers, then rebuild it from those numbers alone.

Reading time ~12 min · all images and numbers come from my trained models

By the end of this chapter you will know

  • what an encoder, a latent representation and a decoder are, and why we compress at all;
  • the three equations behind every autoencoder: \(z=f_\theta(x)\), \(\hat x=g_\phi(z)\) and the reconstruction loss;
  • what a real reconstruction looks like on a brain MRI slice, and how training brings the loss down.

1.The game: describe a picture with only a few numbers

Let's start with a small game. I show you a brain scan, and you are allowed to describe it to your friend over the phone using only 16 numbers. Your friend then has to redraw the scan from those 16 numbers alone.

How would you choose the numbers? You would not waste them on the black background. You would probably say things like "this cut is low in the head, near the eyes", "the brain is big", "the ventricles are dark and wide". In simple words, you would keep what matters and throw away the rest.

An autoencoder is a neural network that learns to play exactly this game, on its own, from thousands of examples. Click through the steps below. Everything in it is real: the slice is a test image the model never saw, the 16 coloured cells are the actual numbers the trained model produced for it, and the output is what the model drew back.

That's the whole idea. The first network, the encoder, squeezes the image into a short list of numbers. The second network, the decoder, tries to rebuild the image from that list. And the only teacher is the image itself: the closer the rebuilt image is to the original, the better.

image x → encoder → latent code z → decoder → reconstruction x̂ → compare with x

Notice what is missing here: labels. Nobody told the model "this slice is near the eyes" or "this person is 60 years old". The image is both the input and the target. That's why autoencoders belong to unsupervised (or self-supervised) learning.

2.Why would anyone want to compress an image?

But why?If the output is just a copy of the input, what did we gain? The answer is: the copy is not the point. The 16 numbers in the middle are the point.

To squeeze 16,384 pixels into 16 numbers, the network has no choice: it has to discover what is common across thousands of brains and describe each brain by how it differs. That short description is called a representation, and learning good representations without labels is one of the oldest goals in machine learning. Here is why it's useful:

  • Compression: store or send 16 numbers instead of 16,384.
  • Denoising: noise is not "common across brains", so a small bottleneck tends to drop it.
  • Anomaly detection: a scan that is unusual reconstructs badly, and a big error is a red flag. This is a very common use in medical imaging.
  • Features for other tasks: the code can feed a classifier when labels are scarce.
  • Generation: if we understand the space of codes, we can create new images. That's what the Variational Autoencoder (chapter 6) is built for.

A bit of history

The idea is older than most of deep learning. In 1986 Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams already trained small networks to copy their input through a narrow hidden layer. Then in 1988 Bourlard and Kamp proved something funny: if the network is purely linear, the best it can do is exactly PCA. So for a while, autoencoders were "PCA with extra steps". In 2006 Hinton and Salakhutdinov showed in Science that a deep, non-linear autoencoder compresses images far better than PCA, and in 2013 Kingma and Welling turned it into a generative model, the VAE. We'll travel that same path in this series.

3.The three parts, one by one

The encoder

The encoder is a function that takes the image and outputs a vector. In our case it's a convolutional network that shrinks the image step by step (chapter 2 opens it up layer by layer).

Encoder => image (128×128) in, short vector z out. It decides what to keep.

The latent representation

The vector z is called the latent code or latent representation. "Latent" just means hidden: these numbers are never given to the model, it invents them. The length of z is the latent dimension, and it is the most important design choice in this whole project. I trained models with 2, 3, 4, 8, 16, 64 and 128 latent numbers.

The decoder

The decoder is the mirror image: it takes z and grows it back into a 128×128 image.

Decoder => short vector z in, image out. It has to imagine everything the code didn't say.

4.The mathematics, in three lines

Now that the picture is clear, the formulas are short. The encoder is a function \(f\) with weights \(\theta\), the decoder a function \(g\) with weights \(\phi\):

$$ z = f_\theta(x) \qquad\qquad \hat{x} = g_\phi(z) = g_\phi\big(f_\theta(x)\big) $$
x is the image, z the latent code, x̂ the reconstruction

To measure how good the reconstruction is, we compare it pixel by pixel. I use the mean squared error over all D = 16,384 pixels:

$$ \mathcal{L}(x,\hat{x}) \;=\; \frac{1}{D}\,\lVert x-\hat{x}\rVert^2 \;=\; \frac{1}{D}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\big(x_i-\hat{x}_i\big)^2 $$
0 means a perfect copy; every pixel is between 0 and 1, so the worst case is 1

And training means finding the weights that make this error small on average over the whole training set:

$$ \theta^*,\phi^* \;=\; \arg\min_{\theta,\phi}\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \mathcal{L}\Big(x^{(n)},\; g_\phi\big(f_\theta(x^{(n)})\big)\Big) $$
solved with gradient descent (AdamW) and backpropagation, one mini-batch of 64 slices at a time

Here, the important thing is that the encoder and the decoder are trained together, by one loss. The encoder only learns what is useful for the decoder, and the decoder only learns to read the encoder's language.

You will often see quality reported as PSNR (peak signal-to-noise ratio) instead of MSE: \(\text{PSNR} = 10\log_{10}(1/\text{MSE})\) in decibels. It's the same information on a log scale; higher is better. An MSE of 0.003 is about 25 dB.

6.Implementation: an autoencoder in PyTorch

Here is the core of my model, trimmed for reading. The full version is in the Auto-Encoders repo.

class Autoencoder(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, latent_dim=16):
        super().__init__()
        self.encoder = Encoder(latent_dim)      # 128x128 image  -> latent_dim numbers
        self.decoder = Decoder(latent_dim)      # latent_dim numbers -> 128x128 image

    def forward(self, x):
        z = self.encoder(x)                     # z = f_theta(x)
        x_hat = self.decoder(z)                 # x_hat = g_phi(z)
        return x_hat, z

# one training step
x_hat, z = model(x)                             # x: a batch of 64 slices, values in [0, 1]
loss = F.mse_loss(x_hat, x)                     # (1/D) * ||x - x_hat||^2, averaged over the batch
optimizer.zero_grad()
loss.backward()                                 # backpropagation through decoder AND encoder
optimizer.step()                                # AdamW, lr 2e-4

7.Why dimensionality reduction matters

An image lives in a space with 16,384 axes, one per pixel. But real brain slices fill only a tiny, curved corner of that space: almost every random 128×128 image is just noise. The set of "plausible brain slices" is called a manifold, and it has far fewer degrees of freedom than 16,384: slice height, head size, ventricle shape, contrast, and so on.

An autoencoder tries to find coordinates for that manifold. With PCA (the linear version) the coordinates have to be straight lines. With a deep, non-linear encoder they can bend along the manifold, which is why a 16-number autoencoder reconstructs far better than 16 principal components.

Limitations to keep in mind. A pixel-wise loss rewards blurry averages, so fine texture is lost first. A good reconstruction score says nothing about clinical usefulness: these models are research tools, not diagnostic ones. And a plain autoencoder has no rule about where codes go, which is why it can't generate new images reliably (chapter 6).

Key takeaways

  • Autoencoder = encoder \(f_\theta\) + bottleneck z + decoder \(g_\phi\), trained to copy the input through the bottleneck.
  • The value is in the bottleneck: it forces the model to learn a compact representation without labels.
  • Loss = mean squared error between input and reconstruction; quality is often reported as PSNR.
  • Small bottlenecks keep the structure and lose the fine detail, so reconstructions look smooth.
